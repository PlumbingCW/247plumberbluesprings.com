How to Plumb a Shower Pan in Blue Springs, MO

A shower pan is only as good as its drain and its test. Here is how the plumbing side of a shower remodel works.

Call (855) 575-2890

By the Blue Springs 24/7 Plumbing team · Published and updated November 14, 2026

A shower pan is the waterproof base of a shower. The plumbing side of it is the drain assembly, the trap, the vent and the connection to the house drain. The tile, the liner and the framing are other trades, but the drain sets the height and the slope that everything else follows.

This guide describes how a shower drain is roughed in, how the pan is tested for leaks and where mistakes happen. It is written for homeowners planning a remodel who want to ask the right questions of their contractor.

It is not a substitute for a licensed plumber on the job. In a house that will be lived in, a leak beneath a tile shower is expensive and slow to discover.

Start with the drain location and slope

The shower drain sits in the floor and connects to a P-trap below, which holds a water seal that blocks sewer gas. The trap arm then runs to a vent and a branch drain. The drain location decides the shower layout, so it is set before the pan is built.

Codes generally require a minimum slope on the pan toward the drain, commonly about a quarter inch per foot, so water runs off instead of pooling. Ask your plumber and your tile installer to confirm the slope and the local rule before the mud bed or liner goes down.

Pan types and their plumbing

Each pan type has a slightly different drain connection.

Pan typeHow it worksPlumbing note
Pre-formed baseOne-piece acrylic or fiberglass panDrain location must match the base.
Mortar mud bed with linerSloped mud over a waterproof linerTwo-part drain clamps the liner.
Foam pan with membranePre-sloped foam under a sheet membraneDrain flange bonds to the membrane.
CurblessFloor slopes to a linear or point drainFloor framing may need to be recessed.

A two-part drain assembly is common for liner pans. It lets water that gets under the tile drain out through weep holes.

Rough-in checklist

A plumber confirms these before anyone closes the floor.

  • The drain is centered where the pan will be.
  • The trap is vented properly.
  • The trap arm slopes toward the branch drain.
  • Supply lines and the valve are set for the correct depth.
  • The valve body is secured and accessible.
  • The rough-in passes a pressure and a drain test.

Access matters. A valve you cannot reach is a repair that means breaking tile.

Flood-test the pan before tile

Before tile goes down, the drain is plugged and the pan is filled with water to just below the curb. The water level is marked and left for several hours, often overnight. If the level drops, the liner or drain connection leaks and must be fixed.

This test is cheap and it is the only way to know a hidden pan is sound. If your contractor skips it, ask for it. A leak found here costs hours. A leak found after tile costs a bathroom.

Common mistakes

The usual errors are a flat pan that pools, a liner that is punctured by a fastener, a drain flange that is not sealed to the liner and a missing vent. A pan that pools grows mold. A punctured liner leaks quietly into the subfloor.

If a shower drains slowly after installation, the trap may be poorly vented or partly blocked. See our drain cleaning page for how we clear and diagnose slow drains.

Water supply side and valve choice

The mixing valve and the supply lines are part of the plumbing rough-in. A pressure-balancing valve helps prevent sudden temperature changes when another fixture runs. The valve depth must match the trim kit and the wall thickness, which is another reason to choose fixtures before the walls close. Plumbing installation covers how we plan remodel rough-in.

Curbs, thresholds and water containment

The curb or threshold is where leaks start if the liner is wrong. The liner must turn up the curb and fasten above the water line, and the corners need folded, not cut, creases. Ask your installer how the corners are handled.

Safety checklist for a shower remodel

Protect people and the house during the work.

  • Shut off water and power to the area before demolition.
  • Wear eye and hand protection around old tile and metal drains.
  • Never work on a gas or electric water heater line without shutting it off.
  • Keep the pan flood test supervised and away from outlets.
  • Confirm permits and licensing with Community Development.

Permits and licensing in Blue Springs

Section 500.130 of the Blue Springs code requires that plumbing permits be issued only with proof of a valid license, and it describes limited owner-occupant exemptions on a single-family home. Ask Community Development what a shower remodel needs in your case.

We do not state the local code slope or vent requirements from memory. Ask the city or your licensed plumber for the current adopted plumbing code.

Questions about this topic in Blue Springs

Do I need a permit to build a new shower in Blue Springs?

It depends on the scope. Ask Community Development, and know that Section 500.130 ties permits to proof of license.

How long should the flood test run?

Many installers run it for several hours or overnight. Follow your inspector and installer guidance.

Can I reuse the old drain?

Sometimes. The trap, vent and slope have to meet current requirements, so a plumber should inspect them.

What is a curbless shower?

A shower with a floor that slopes to the drain with no raised edge. It needs extra floor framing work.

Why does my new shower drain slowly?

A partial blockage, an incorrectly vented trap or a flat pan. A plumber can test each.
